A merchant arrived by boat in a distant land and hired one of the porters to bring his bags to the inn. A short while later, the porter arrived, out of breath and dripping with sweat. He asked for payment, but the merchant confidently responded, “You didn't bring the right bags.” Outraged, the porter asked how he could possibly know such a thing when he hadn't even seen the packages yet. The merchant answered calmly that his bags were small sacks full of diamonds and other precious gems – if he had brought the correct packages there was no way that he would be sweating so much.

This story is a parable from the great Maggid of Dubno, in explanation of the following verse (Isaiah 43): “It is not to Me (G-d) that you are calling, for Israel has grown weary.” The verse begs an explanation; what does weariness have to do with calling out to G-d? But with this parable we can understand the message. Hashem told the Jewish people that His service is meant to be a pleasure. Learning Torah and doing other mitzvot is like collecting diamonds, service which we should be itching for the opportunity to fulfill. If “Israel has grown weary” then clearly their service of G-d has taken the wrong approach and “it is not to Me that you are calling.”

If we would only realize the tremendous opportunity afforded to us with each of the Mitzvot, we would never grow weary no matter how much we work. Picture a man who has just been granted twenty-four hours in the King's treasury. He is allowed to keep anything he gathers in that time period, but when the clock runs out the game is over. You can imagine how hard he would work, not resting for a moment to eat or to sleep. For twenty-four hours he would be the hardest-working individual in the world. But if you'd ask him how he's doing, would he respond “Oy! This work is so difficult!” Of course not! He understands his incredible good fortune and would revel every second that he was allotted.

Every Mitzva is an opportunity to achieve greatness of character, closeness to G-d, and immeasurable rewards, as well as attain true happiness. Mitzvot can be hard work, they can be demanding, and they can even be expensive, but if we would realize what an opportunity they are, we would never grow weary from the service of Hashem.

In this week's Parhsa, Bilam praises Israel with the following words: “I did not see toil in Israel.” Isn't this is a puzzling way to praise someone – it sounds more like an insult, calling them lazy and unwilling to work? The Ohr Hachaim explains the phrase to mean that even though the Jews expend great effort hard work in the service of G-d, it does not seem to them to be a toil. They realize the great value of Torah and mitzvot and thereby they are ecstatic about their good fortune and relish every opportunity spend in the King's treasury.

I'd like to close with a true story about Rabbi Chaim Kanievsky, he should live and be well. Rabbi Kanievsky is famed as one of the greatest Torah scholars of our generation and one who does not waste a single second; he is constantly engrossed in Torah. When he was a young man, one of his peers noticed that although he'd come to the Kollel (study hall) with bloodshot eyes every day, clearly running on very little sleep, he never once dozed off while learning. One day, however, a man approached Rabbi Kanievsky with a newspaper clipping – something important but not Torah related. The Rabbi began to read the newspaper clipping, and within seconds his eyes closed in exhaustion. This is our point in case. The Rabbi was completely exhausted from hard work and lack of sleep, yet he was so excited about the Torah that he never found it wearying. Only when he tried to focus on something relatively mundane did his fatigue finally catch up with him and knock him out within seconds.

We all were privy to see Uncle Yonatan's great passion for anything Torah related. He never tired for a second from learning something new. He would walk miles to do a mitzvah, and in prison he would prepare for months in advance to be able to once eat matzah or sit in a succah. His great love for G-d and his Torah made him indefatigable. “Mighty waters could not extinguish his love.”

May we all merit an appreciation of the great treasure that every mitzvah truly is and thereby experience all the joy that Judaism has to offer.
