There once was a prince, the only son of a great and mighty monarch, who began to stray from the good ways of his kindly father. In his youth, his rebelliousness was relatively harmless, but as he grew the problems grew with him. His father warned him many a time, but to no avail. Left without a choice, the king threatened to beat the prince if he would not change his ways. Unfortunately, the threat wasn’t good enough and soon the king had no choice but to order lashes upon his beloved son’s back. The king begged and pleaded with his son not to make him repeat the order, and prayed that this one-time measure should succeed in saving the future of his child. But still, the prince wouldn’t break. He continued in his ways, and was beaten again. Time after time, the story repeated itself; the beating, the prayers and entreaties, and the subsequent brazenness on behalf of the prince, who refused to right his wrongs. Once, after yet another flagrancy, the king took a merciful look at his son. The prince, once strong and handsome, was now black and blue from the beatings, thin, pale, and limping. The king realized that his son wouldn’t be able to survive another beating, but at the same time he couldn’t allow the misdeeds to go unpunished. He couldn’t just allow his son to go lost.

After some thought, the king had an idea. If his dear child couldn’t take the beatings, then the king would bear the lashes on his own back. The prince would watch the beating, knowing that those blows were meant for himself, but the king would accept them out of love for his son. In this way, the king hoped, his son would finally capitulate, unable to cause such suffering to his great father…

Rav Shimshon Pincus explains that this is a parable to our present-day exile of the twenty-first century. In the days of old, the children of Israel, the only son of Hashem, began to stray. He warned us and foretold of the punishments which would come upon us if we wouldn’t correct our ways. He sent us leaders and prophets which pleaded on His behalf that we repent, but to no avail. Finally, we forced Hashem’s hand and He ordered us to be beaten and punished, hoping to save us from going lost. The beatings we suffered were harsh and painful, much worse than we can possibly conjure up in our imagination. The destruction of the Beit Hamikdash with rivers of Jewish blood. The expulsions and libels, the Inquisition, the Cossacks, the crusades, and the holocaust. And these are just a drop in the ocean.

We suffered them all, and perhaps we made slight amendments, but ultimately, we stood brazen and bold – unwilling to change.

Today, we live lives of freedom and prosperity. Things aren’t perfect, but we are a very far way from the tortures and torment which our people have lived through. When we read stories of the holocaust or the inquisition, we can barely muster the courage to read on, and we certainly can’t imagine being able to withstand such trials. We wouldn’t last for a minute in the face of what our ancestors stood up to.

We are the prince who, after generations of beatings, can no longer survive the blows. We are weak, tired, and worn; scarcely mustering up the strength to withstand our small tests.

Our loving Father cannot hit us any more, because one more blow would finish us off. But He also cannot allow us to stray without consequence. So, He has taken the whip off of our backs and placed it, so to speak, upon His own. Instead of striking us with the Nazi inferno, He strikes himself with the millions of Jews who don’t even know His name. And instead of striking us with the tortures of the Inquisition, he strikes himself with so many of his children who have gone lost amongst the nations.

The question is how we are going to react. Will we breathe a sigh of relief that we are no longer suffering and continue in our ways? Will we sit by in comfort as our Father suffers for our deeds and allow Him to continue bearing the brunt of our actions?

Or will we finally say enough is enough? Will we finally decide that we cannot be the cause of our Father’s pain, and thereby commit to better ourselves?

Will we be the ones to say, “Daddy, take the rod off Your holy shoulders – we’re coming home now.”

Good Shabbos,

Yosef
