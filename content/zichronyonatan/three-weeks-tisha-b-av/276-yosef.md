This week, we began the 'three weeks' of mourning for the Beis Hamikdash. These begin with the fast of Shiva Asar B'Tamuz, and conclude with the fast of Tisha B'Av. Throughout this period, there are many rabbinically ordained practices of mourning; creating the feeling that the loss of Beis Hamikdash is as painful to us as the loss of a close relative. Most of these laws are ascending in their severity as we draw closer to Tisha B'Av, with the lowest level for the remainder of the month of Tamuz, a higher level with the onset of the month of Av, yet another level with the week of Tisha B'Av, and culminating with the fast and heightened affliction of Tisha B'Av itself. Curiously though, there is one exception to the rule of a gradual increase; we start off the very first day with a fast although no fasting is required for the rest of the interim. What is the meaning of this break in the order of things?

Let's face it. Mourning the Beis Hamikdash is perhaps one of the hardest things for us American Jews to properly perform in the entire Jewish calendar. As hard as we try, the loss of the Beis Hamikdash is just not something we easily relate to. It seems so distant and so far from reality.

I believe one of the reasons we find this so difficult is because we are so comfortable here in 'exile'. Throughout our entire sojourn amongst the nations we have been met with hardship, persecution, and poverty. These last few decades in America have truly been a time of blessing and prosperity,  not only in a material sense but also in our ability to freely practice our Judaism. And yet, this greatest of blessings carries with it a very difficult test – the test of complacency.

Our ancestors had no problem relating to the pain of exile; they felt it keenly with the hunger in their stomachs and the hatred of their neighbors. It was clear as day that Poland, Turkey, Russia, and Egypt, were not our homes, and so our fathers longed with all their hearts for Moshiach and the rebuilding of the Beis Hamikdash.

Not so here in America. We may know intellectually that as Jews we belong in Israel of old, but it is very far from our hearts. In our state of security, affluence, and societal acceptance, we feel much too comfortable and much too at home in America. We feel as if we belong, and this is a terrible mistake. Yes, we should feel a tremendous amount of gratitude to America, and yes we should care and even pray for the welfare of the the country, but it is not our home and not our place. We belong in a much higher plane; in a society governed by G-d Himself and among a people driven exclusively towards spirituality. If we don't feel this way and we don't long for more, then we are in need of a real wake-up call.

That wake-up call is what our sages intended by all the customs of the three weeks. We begin with minor things such as not listening to music and not cutting our hair. These disrupt our normal way of things to a certain degree, but we can easily shrug them off and go on with life. So then we step it up a notch; during the nine days of Av we cannot bathe in hot water, wear freshly laundered clothing, or eat meat. These are pretty hard to ignore and really demand a certain amount of our attention, but they still do not encompass the entire day and one could go about most of his days without thought about what is going on. But on Tisha B'Av even the most callous and unthinking individual is compelled to realize that something is wrong. He cannot eat, drink, sleep in his normal way, sit on a chair, wear shoes, and so on. This puts a complete halt on life as usual and all but forces us to wake up, at least for the day.

I think that perhaps this is the purpose of starting the three weeks with a fast. Our sages wanted to shake us form our reverie and disrupt things in a way that couldn't be missed right from the get-go. Once we're awake from the fast of Shiva Asar B'Tamuz, we can then ease into things with gradually more severe practices, while still taking their message.

It would seem that this year, besides for our sages enactments, we are being given a healthy dose of divine assistance in this area. Hashem has made it easier than ever to realize that all is not well, that we do not belong here, and that there is so much more to long for.

First, Hashem brought every world power to its knees with a tiny virus which has forced us to realize that we don't have things under control. He saw to it that so many things that we take for granted were suddenly cast into doubt. Can we get whatever we need? Can we go where we want? Can we rely on our medical expertise? Questions which we might have answered in the affirmative are suddenly greeted with a very loud 'No!'

And then we experienced the riots. Here in Los Angeles we happened to have box seats on the event, so my experience may be a little more extreme then your memories, but the bottom line is the same. On May 30th, just after Shavuos, I sat in my house listening to absolute pandemonium outside. Smoke filled the air, explosions sounded rapidly, cars wizzed by, sirens and helicopters were everywhere. I tried leaving my house to daven Maariv a block away – but I quickly ran back inside as something exploded nearby. In the morning we saw the damage everywhere. Nearly every store for miles had been smashed and looted, and the city had a curfew for the rest of the week.

Who would have thought that here in America I would be scared to walk out my front door? We're so used to our security and comfort, but now that was stripped from us. We suddenly see clearly that we are not safe and secure here; that all is not well.

How much more do we need to wake us up? How much will it take to shake us from our complacency?

Let's not wait any longer, but take the medicine and use the opportunity to engrave in our hearts the message of the three weeks. Let us feel the gaping hole in our existence, truly pray and hope for redemption, and ultimately merit our final homecoming.

Good Shabbos,

Yosef
