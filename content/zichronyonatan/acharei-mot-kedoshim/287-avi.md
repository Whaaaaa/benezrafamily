This week' double Parsha Achrei & Kedoshim summarize the Yom Kippur procedure. In wake of the death of Nadav and Avihu, Achrei opens with warning the Cohen Gadol when entering the Holy Of Holies. As part of the Yom Kippur service, we are told to draw lots to determine which of two lambs is to be sacrificed and which is to be sent to the edge of the desert.

Later on, in the parsha, it mentions that no animal sacrifices be made outside the Beit Hamikdash. The prohibition of consuming blood and the laws of sexual morality are also mentioned.

The parsha Kedoshim begins with ‘you shall be holy, for I, the L‑rd your G‑d, am holy.” Following is a list of core traits, monogamy, charity, equality in law, Shabbat, honesty in business, respect and awe for parents, and sanctity of life. These traits separate and uplift the Jewish nation. Kedoshim leaves us off with the famous quote “love your fellow as yourself’. In other words “the whole Torah on one foot.” Or as Rabbi Akiva put it the “cardinal principle”

Rabbi Akiva himself remains a pillar in this thought, however, his students were not able to exemplify loving one's fellow as themselves. Rabbi Akiva's students were diligent and exacting, each with their unique thoughts and talents. However many were killed by the plague for “they did not conduct themselves with respect for each other.” How could Rabbi Akiva's instruction lead them so far astray?

As individuals, we are encouraged to make note of our accomplishments and amass self-worth, while constantly looking for ways to refine ourselves and grow. Growth is supposed to be a proactive process driven by desire and willingness. Often our personal change is only a reaction to having repeated a behavior until it can no longer be sustained. The change that results from this process didn't stem from a true change in the will. True growth is achieved only through constant refining of the will and proactive decisions to change your own behavior.

Rabbi Akiva's students were like brothers engrossed in the same topics and living side by side. Our sages have said that “just as every person’s face differs from the faces of his fellows, so to every person’s mind differs from the minds of his fellows.” This would have been of minor concern if Rabbi Akiva's students didn't love each other so much. Each individual loved their fellow to the level they loved themselves leaving no room for nuance, and no room for opinion. Students swallowed each other whole correcting and refining each other, from a third party these students did not look like friends at all.

Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai lived past the plague that wiped out 24,000 of R Akiva's students. When the Roman empire put a price on his head he and his son Elazar went to a cave. For twelve years they studied Torah, and when they emerged the sight of mundane tasks at hand sent fury and fire onto day laborers. When a voice called from heaven “Have you come out to destroy My world? Return to your cave!” Rabbi Shimon returned for his thirteenth year of cave dwelling in which he prepared himself to invest in the endeavors of others. Now when he reintegrated, his gaze gave blessing and healing.

Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai is revered for being the one for whom Torah was his sole vocation. In his thirteenth year, he attached himself to an eternity that is Torah and learned to advocate for the same devotion in others. In putting more emphasis on practice and learning Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai only led by example. In contrast to the 24,000 students who held their fellow to their own standard leaving no room for growth in their community.

This week, on the 33rd of a forty-nine day period counting the days between Pesach and Shavuot, we will mourn the loss of 24,000 students of R Akiva. We will also celebrate the life of R’ Shimon bar Yochai who delicately balanced the ability to recognize the virtues of others and encourage growth by leading as an example. A true mark of a tzaddik is one whose death inspires their largest movement. This is certainly true of R Shimon bar Yochai.
