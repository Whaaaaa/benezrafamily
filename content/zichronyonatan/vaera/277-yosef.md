The weekly reading of the Torah has a profound effect on the world, and therefore we are very careful never to finish a reading with something negative. In fact, the two longest aliya's are the ones containing the Torah's curses – we go on and on until we find something positive to close with.

Why then, on Monday and Thursday of Parshat Va'eira do we finish the second reading with the seemingly negative phrase 'and the people did not listen to Moshe because of shortness of spirit and hard labor'? Here the Torah describes the hopelessness of the Jewish people, who were so downtrodden and broken that they could not accept the message of salvation. Does this not violate the principle of always finding a good ending?

The answer, says Rabbi E. B. Finkel, is that the sorry state of the Jews and the unbearable oppression which they were subjected to were indeed the catalyst for their early redemption. Viewed from this lens, the verse is ultimately a good thing, not worse than the painful procedure performed to save the life of one who is ill.

This brings us to the very poignant picture painted by Rabbi Shalom Scwhadron. He describes how, from the tortures of Egypt, the Jews screamed out to Hashem for salvation. We can't even imagine what a cry that was, as they poured out their heart to their loving Father to save the from the most terrible conditions known to man. What feeling, what concentration, and what trust must have been contained in that prayer! And yet, they immediately found themselves facing yet another decree, as Pharaoh ruthlessly determined that they would no longer be given the straw with which to make bricks, but would have to gather it themselves. This on top of their already insurmountable workload. Can we imagine how they must have felt? Here they truly had prayed their hearts out to Hashem, and not only were they not answered, but they were stricken once again. Could one possibly feel more forsaken? Surely Hashem must have totally abandoned them, uninterested in his relationship with the Jewish people!

But in truth, explains Rabbi Schwadron, they had been heard, they had been answered, and this very trial was the key to the salvation for which they had begged. There was a certain amount of suffering which the Jewish people needed to be subjected to in order to form them into the nation they would one day become. There was no way around it – it simply had to be. This was set at four-hundred years of Egyptian bondage. But when Hashem heard their prayers and saw that they could not possibly retain their identity for such a long time; He decided to end the sentence early. But what would be with the suffering they needed in order to achieve their identity? It couldn't be skipped, so it would be concentrated, effectively delivering four-hundred years of pain in the span of two-hundred and ten years. A terribly difficult period in our history, but one which we could survive and from which we would emerge still stronger and prepared to accept the Torah.

This means that the intense suffering which they endured after crying out to Hashem was none other than the answer to their prayers. While they may have felt forsaken, they were actually already experiencing the answer to their problems.

The same is true for us. The divine master-plan is far far beyond the understanding of our mortal minds, and we don't know the reasons for most of the things that happen to us. Life is no cup of tea, and it is often fraught with heartache and challenges. Indeed, sometimes we are cast into utter darkness with no ray of light, and hopefully our reaction is to cry out to our loving Father for help. Even then, we often don't hear Him answer, and we may even feel that we are worse off than we started. At those times, let us remember how our fathers in Egypt must have felt the same way, and let us understand that, just as then, what we perceive as more misfortune may really be paving the way for the grandest of salvations.

Good Shabbos,

Yosef
