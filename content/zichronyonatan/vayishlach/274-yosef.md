After a 34-year stint away from home, Yaakov finally decides to return to the land of Canaan. He remains fearful of a confrontation with his brother Esav, who has vowed to kill him. And of course, as soon as word gets out that Yaakov is coming home, it is none other than Esav, surrounded by an army, who goes to 'greet' him. In an effort to appease his brother, Yaakov sends a number of lavish and opulent gifts ahead, hoping that Esav will take the bribe and call off his murderous plans.

This all sounds like a very sound and logical way to avoid a conflict, but the Torah's wording throws in an element of the supernatural. Instead of saying that he sent the gifts with 'shluchim' or messengers, it says that he sent them with 'malachim' – literally translated as angels. And indeed, according to one opinion in the Talmud, these were no ordinary men but actual angels which Yaakov sent to be his emissaries.

This needs an explanation. We have a general rule to avoid the miraculous except when it is absolutely necessary. When a tzadik has a miracle performed on his behalf without a true need, it costs him dearly from his heavenly dividends. Why would Yaakov have tapped into the miraculous, at such a premium, when he could have sent ordinary men?

The answer, says Rabbi Moshe Feinstein, lies in a Talmudic story about Rebbi Chanina ben Dosa, which Uncle Yonatan used to constantly recount:

One Friday afternoon, he (Rebbi Chanina ben Dosa) found his daughter upset. He asked her, “Why are you sad?” She said, “Because I mixed up a jug of oil with a jug of vinegar and I poured the vinegar into the Shabbos candles.” He responded, “What's the matter? He who told oil to light will tell the vinegar to light.” And the candles burned the entire Shabbos until they were able to make havdala with that flame.

To Rebbi Chanina, burning was no natural and necessary quality of oil. He perceived that the only thing allowing the oil to light was Hashem's decree that it do so. It didn't have to be that way, it wasn't natural, it was simply a miracle which Hashem performs on a regular basis. With such a perception, he was no less astounded by this daily miracle than by an 'unnatural' miracle. Both are no more and no less than the will of Hashem.

Because Rebbi Chanina perceived Hashem's involvement in every 'fact' of 'nature' so clearly, an open miracle was no bigger an event. This, says Rabbi Feinstein, was Yaakov's perspective as well. He saw the hand of G-d in every facet of life and thereby felt no less fascination at the ability to send men than the ability to send angels. So why not? Because of his exalted level of belief in G-d, it cost no more to send angels than to send men.

With Chanukah just around the corner, we can use this to answer one of the most well-known questions about Chanukah. We know that the flask of oil which was found was enough to burn for only one day, and because it miraculously lit for eight days we have eight days of Chanukah. The question is that since there was naturally enough oil for one day it comes out that there were only seven days of miracles. Why do we celebrate eight days for a seven-day miracle?

According to what we have learned, we can answer very simply that the fact that the oil burnt for one day is really no more miraculous than the other seven. It is for this reason that our sages declared eight days of celebration, in appreciation of the miraculous reality of nature.

This is a lesson for all of us to realize that Hashem's will is the only thing powering the world and all of the 'natural' miracles which we witness all around us. Nothing has to be, nothing is by chance, and nothing but nothing is beyond His reach.

We're in good hands.
