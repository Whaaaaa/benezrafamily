The custom of breaking the middle matzo at the Seder is one that has many explanations, but I'd like to share a particularly beautiful explanation that I heard not long ago from Rav Uren Reich.

There were once two friends who grew up together in one of the shtetl's of Europe. From childhood and until their perspective marriages, the two were inseparable. They were closer than brothers, they understood each other like nobody else, and through thick and thin they were there for each other. But the day finally came that they both became engaged to girls from far away towns. As was the custom in those days, they would both live in the town of their in-laws, with a great distance between them. Essentially, they understood that they would have no contact for years on end, and they were greatly saddened by the prospect.

In an effort to seal their friendship to defy time and space, they resolved to hire an artist to paint a portrait of each friend. In this way, they would always be remembered. But instead of each friend taking the picture of his fellow, they decided to cut each portrait in half, each of them retaining half of their own picture and half of their friend's. This would symbolize that each of them was indivisible from their friend. Reuven without Shimon wasn't Reuven, and Shimon without Reuven wasn't Shimon. Neither could be complete without each other.

This is what we are doing by breaking the middle matzo. One half represents Hashem, and one half represents the Jewish people. In this way, we show that our relationship is no ordinary friendship of two disparate parties. No, ours is a union so deep that we cannot be divided. Without Hashem we are not a people, and, so to speak, Hashem without the Jews is also incomplete.

Yes, there are exiles, holocausts, and terribly bitter times. There are times when we are broken and cracked, aching for the embrace of our our Beloved. But even in such times, we are not just the broken parts of ourselves, but a broken half of one united whole with Hashem.

Have a happy and kosher Pesach,

Yosef
