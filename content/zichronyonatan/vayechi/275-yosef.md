When Yaakov Avinu passed away, Yosef and the brothers escorted him on his last journey to be buried in the land of Canaan. There, in the middle of the funeral, an unwanted guest popped in. It was Esav, threatening to disrupt the entire burial and insisting that the final plot left in Maarat Hamachpeilah was rightfully his and not Yaakov's. A debate ensued, with Yaakov's children stating that Esav had sold his plot years before to Yaakov. Esav insisted that they prove it with the sale document, which was all the way back in Egypt. Naftali, known for his alacrity, was elected to run back and get the document, but in the mean time Yaakov's body was in a state of dishonor; unburied for so long. Everyone was upset about the situation, but they felt they had no choice but to wait for Naftali to procure the document and prove their case. Everyone but Chushim, the deaf son of Don. Chushim watched what was going on but, due to his deafness, couldn't understand what they were waiting for. He asked and was told simply that Esav was holding things up until Naftali would return. Chushim wondered how it could be that they would leave his grandfather in a state of dishonor for such nonsense and decided to take matters into his own hands. Without any questions, he promptly picked up a stick and knocked Esav over the head, enabling the funeral procession to continue.1Gemara Sotah 13a

What was it that enabled Chushim to act with such confidence and cool even when his uncles, some of history's greatest men, were paralyzed into inaction? What gave him the clarity to realize that there was no need to dance to Esav's beat and thereby defile the body of Yaakov?

The answer is that Chushim was deaf. Yosef and the brothers were caught up in a debate with Esav, with each party trying to prove the point. Just by becoming embroiled in the debate with Esav, they already saw a little bit of his side of the story, and they were therefore unable to step out of the argument and perceive the reality of the situation. But not Chushim. Chushim couldn't hear any of Esav's arguments, he never entered the conversation, and so he was able to remain unmoved and to set things straight.

It is well-known that Esav was not just a historical figure, but rather an embodiment of the Yetzer Hora (Evil Inclination) himself, who lives to this day in the heart of every person. The story of Chushim is likewise not just a piece of history, but marching orders for all of us in our battle against that same Yetzer Hora. If we want to win the battle against this most cunning and sweet-talking enemy, the best course of action is to never enter the conversation. When our Yetzer Hora speaks up and tries to entice us to do something we know is wrong, let us know that just by entering the debate we have already lost half the war. When we start considering why not to do it, when we begin compiling a case for the side of good – that's already the beginning of the end. If we want to win the battle we have to end it abruptly and forcefully, never allowing the Yetzer Hora an audience with whom to engage.

To illustrate, I'd like to share just one example that I think we all can relate to. You decide at night that you must wake up early the next morning to do some act of good. You set an alarm (or three) and climb into bed, ready to take on the world the coming day. The next thing you know (and it can't possibly have been more than an hour or two) your alarm is ringing – time to face the test and get yourself out of bed. But somebody (who doesn't seem to ever sleep) is there in your ear, telling you that this is a bad idea. After all, you're exhausted, you barely slept last night, and besides, you're in the middle of a great dream.

At this point one of two things can happen. The first: You're a good person and you truly want to do the right thing, so you're not going to go down without a fight. You answer back, with all the authority you can muster, that tired or not I have work to get done and...... Usually you don't finish the sentence before you've dozed back off and, ultimately, lost the fight.

That's the almost inevitable result of conversing, even arguing, with the Yetzer Hora.

The second scenario is for the student of Chushim: You hear the Yetzer Hora trying to engage you in conversation, so you end it before it starts by bolting upright and hitting him over the head with your pillow. No conversation, no debate, and no room to fall in.

May we all learn from Chushim when to make ourselves deaf and how to knock the Yetzer Hora out of our lives once and for all.
